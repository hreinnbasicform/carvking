Carvking The animal they would not draw 3 October 2026

Carvking · images · natural history

The oldest panda drawing got the coat right and the animal wrong.

For two thousand years Chinese texts described a black-and-white bamboo-eating bear. The pictures described a chimera with an elephant trunk. That split is not a slip. It is the archive.

Record first. Theories after. The pictures are real. The reading is the argument.

The oldest picture

The oldest surviving illustration of the animal the Chinese called mo (貘) sits in the Erya yintu, an illustrated edition of the ancient glossary Erya. The transmitted pictures come from a Song or Yuan drawing, surviving as an 1801 facsimile published by Zeng Yu from Song-period prints. It is the only early example that shows the coat the way a panda coat actually works: white through the middle, dark at the shoulders and legs.

Everything else is wrong. The head has a trunk. Later Chinese and Japanese blocks follow the ninth-century recipe: elephant trunk, rhinoceros eyes, cow tail, tiger paws. That recipe is not a field note. It is a poem. Bo Juyi, the Tang official, wrote of a painting of a mo beside his bed and described exactly that composite. Images of it were supposed to repel contagion and evil. The charm outlived the animal.

The absence

There is no known drawing of a giant panda from life before the nineteenth century. Father Armand David sent the animal to European science in 1869. The first printed image followed in a French zoological book in 1874. The giant panda does not appear on ancient bronzes or pottery. It is not a subject of Tang poetry, Song painting, Yuan drama, Ming fiction, or Qing porcelain. It enters “traditional” Chinese art only in the late twentieth century.

That is odd, because the animal was not unknown to people who could have ordered a picture. Emperor Taizong of Tang handed out mo pelts as banquet gifts. A third-century record has an emperor at Chang’an keeping several as pets. The Dowager Empress Bo, buried around 170 BCE, went into the tomb with a panda skull. Luxury fur, palace pets, a grave good. Then two millennia of artists who somehow never drew the thing they were wrapping emperors in.

The words were better than the pictures

Xu Shen, around the first and second centuries, compared mo to a bear in the Shuowen jiezi. Every description before Bo Juyi does the same. The Erya calls it a white-and-black animal that eats bamboo. Li Shizhen, in the sixteenth century, puts an aggressive yellow-and-black bamboo-eater in Sichuan. Donald Harper’s study in Early China tracks the name back to the giant panda and pins the tapir mix-up on Jean-Pierre Abel-Rémusat in 1824, who trusted the trunked woodblocks and renamed the word.

So the file splits. Elites who read got a bear. Everyone who looked at a picture got a monster that wards off plague. Abel-Rémusat then exported the monster to Western zoology, and mo became the modern Chinese and Japanese word for tapir. The original referent was vacated. A name that once pointed at a panda now points at an animal that did not live in historical China.

The other panda, found as an otter

Europe’s first picture of the red panda is a watercolour of about 1820, painted by an unnamed Indian artist for Thomas Hardwicke and filed as “Mustela Zebellina? From Nepal” — a sable, with a question mark. The specimen trail starts in a Kathmandu bazaar in 1818, a “stuffed otter” noticed by Bharat Singh, one of Nathaniel Wallich’s collectors. Hardwicke’s paper was read at the Linnean Society in 1821 and published in 1827. Frédéric Cuvier described and named Ailurus fulgens in 1825, from specimens that likely came through the same Nepali network via Alfred Duvaucel. The red panda got the English word “panda” almost fifty years before the giant panda was formally named.

What the pictures are doing

Four readings. The first two are the ordinary historical explanation. The last two are the ones that do not sit still.

Theory 1 · curation, not ignorance

A creature you cannot draw cannot be claimed.

The coat is the one detail a copyist would not invent, because a charm does not need a white belly. Someone had seen the animal. Then the head was replaced. Accurate sentences kept moving through dictionaries. The public image was a ward against disease. Ask who benefits from a panda that looks like a myth. A classified animal can be hunted, taxed, gifted, and studied. A charm cannot.

Theory 2 · the two-thousand-year blank

Rarity does not explain a palace pet with no portrait.

Chinese artists drew tigers, cranes, cicadas, horses, elephants. They drew bears. They did not draw this bear. If the pelt was rare enough to be an imperial gift, it was rare enough to be worth a picture. The blank is selective. Either nobody who painted ever saw one, which contradicts the pets and the grave, or the form was kept out of the image record on purpose.

Theory 3 · the naming theft

The lesser animal got the name first. The paper was late on purpose, or the delay was useful.

A stuffed otter in a bazaar. An unnamed painter. A mislabeled sheet. A reading in 1821 and a publication in 1827, just late enough for Paris to take the name. Too many hands between the mountain and the journal. The red panda is not a raccoon and not a bear, and it is not close kin to the giant panda. Convergent thumbs, convergent diet, and then a shared English name granted to the animal Europe met first. The giant one arrives already nicknamed after a cousin it does not have.

Theory 4 · the animal that hides from painters

In 2021 the coat was shown to work as camouflage. It had already worked on the archive.

Tim Caro and colleagues, using wild photographs and predator vision models, found the black patches sit with trunks and shadow, the white with foliage and snow, and the outline breaks up past about fifty-five metres. An animal that disappears in its own forest also disappeared from the only civilisation that lived next to it. Two thousand years of not being drawn is a second kind of camouflage. The question is what else in that forest was filed as a charm instead of a species.

The record is plain. The texts knew a bear. The blocks knew a monster. Europe met the red one as an otter and a sable, and met the giant one only after the name had been given away. If that is just how premodern natural history works, it is a strange way for it to work on one animal and not the tiger.
