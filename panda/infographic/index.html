The Mo Chronicle

2,000 Years of the Animal They Would Not Draw

A chart of two pandas. The texts described the giant panda accurately for two thousand years; the pictures turned it into a monster with a trunk. Follow the arrows from the oldest glossary to the 2021 camouflage study, and watch where the record cracks.

The Chronicle, entry by entry

Every node on the chart, in order. Solid line: documented. Dashed: delayed, broken or suppressed.

I · The giant-panda track · the old empire

  1. c. 3rd c. BCE

    The Erya names the mo

    China's oldest glossary (its core usually dated to about the 3rd century BCE) defines the mo 貘 as a “white leopard.” Guo Pu's commentary (276–324) fills it in: bear-like, small head, mottled black and white, eats bamboo.

  2. c. 170–155 BCE

    A panda skull in an empress's tomb

    Excavated in 1975 from the tomb of Empress Dowager Bo near Xi'an. Popular accounts date the burial to c. 170 BCE; she died in 155 BCE. Only the skull was found.

  3. c. 100–149 CE

    Xu Shen compares it to a bear

    In the Shuowen jiezi, China's first great character dictionary, Xu Shen (c. 55–c. 149) writes that the mo “resembles the bear, yellow and black, comes from Shu” (Sichuan).

  4. 210 CE (reported)

    Pandas in the palace?

    Widely repeated: an emperor around 210 CE kept several mo as pets. No primary source found, so treat it as unproven. Better attested: Sima Xiangru (c. 138 BCE) lists the mo in the Han emperor's hunting park.

  5. 626–649

    Pelts as banquet gifts

    Emperor Taizong of Tang hands out mo pelts to favoured officials. Li Shizhen later notes the mo was “a favorite motif for screens” in the Tang.

  6. 823 · Bo Juyi (772–846)

    Bo Juyi's screen: the corruption point

    Corruption point

    The poet-official has a mo painted on his folding screen to ward off illness. His preface fixes the formula: elephant trunk, rhinoceros eyes, cow tail, tiger paws. From here the record splits: the texts keep the bear, the pictures keep the monster.

The text branch · accurate

  1. 1596

    The texts stay accurate

    Li Shizhen's Bencao gangmu: like a bear, small head, short legs, black and white, eats bamboo, lives on Mount Emei in Sichuan. Then, in the same entry, he repeats the trunk.

  2. 1970s

    The correction that took 150 years

    Chinese scholars, starting with the zoologist Gao Yaoting in 1973, show that the ancient mo was the giant panda. Abel-Rémusat's 1824 tapir error is overturned. Donald Harper (Early China, 2013) makes the full case.

The image branch · corrupted

  1. 960–1279 · Song

    The only early image with the right coat

    Break from reality

    An illustrated Song edition of the Erya: white middle, black shoulders and legs, and an elephant-like head and trunk. It survives only through later copies.

  2. 1271–1368 · Yuan

    Copied by hand, then copied again

    A Yuan-dynasty manuscript copies the Song edition. That copy survives as the 1801 woodblock facsimile now on Wikimedia Commons.

  3. 1609

    The chimera goes to print

    Wang Qi's illustrated encyclopedia Sancai tuhui prints the trunked mo, and the copying begins.

  4. 1712 · Japan

    Japan copies it

    Terajima Ryōan's Wakan sansai zue reproduces the image; Harper uses the raised left front paw to show the copying.

  5. 1725 · China

    So does the imperial encyclopedia

    The vast Qing compendium Gujin tushu jicheng copies it too. The corrupted image now spans two empires.

The image reaches Europe, and the correction

  1. 1824

    The error becomes “science”

    Scientific error

    The Paris sinologist Jean-Pierre Abel-Rémusat looks at the trunked woodblocks and decides the mo is the newly described Malayan tapir. Western zoology, then Japan and China, follow. Mo still means tapir today.

  2. 1869

    The first specimen reaches Europe

    The missionary Père Armand David obtains a giant panda from Sichuan hunters and sends it to Paris, recording the local name bai xiong (white bear). He calls it Ursus melanoleucus.

  3. 1874

    The first printed picture

    A plate in Henri and Alphonse Milne-Edwards' Recherches pour servir à l'histoire naturelle des mammifères (1868–1874), drawn from David's specimen.

  4. 1941–42

    A panda on a fighter plane

    Reportedly the first panda logo: the American Volunteer Group (“Flying Tigers”), flying from Kunming from December 1941, had a squadron nicknamed the “Panda Bears,” with a panda insignia.

  5. mid/late 20th c.

    Two thousand years late to the scroll

    The giant panda appears in “traditional” ink painting for the first time, roughly 40–50 years ago, after it becomes a national symbol.

  6. 1983 (reported)

    From song to literature

    Reportedly the panda becomes a literary motif in China in the 1980s, after a popular song. The exact song and date are hard to pin down; treat this one loosely.

II · The red-panda track

  1. 1818

    A stuffed otter that isn't

    Bharat Singh, a plant collector in Kathmandu for the Calcutta Botanic Garden, spots a “stuffed otter” in the bazaar. It is a red panda (Watson, Archives of Natural History, 2023).

  2. c. 1820

    The first picture Europe sees

    An unknown Indian artist paints the animal for Thomas Hardwicke, reportedly labelled “Mustela Zebellina? From Nepal.” Almost certainly the first red-panda image to reach Europe (Lowther, 2021).

  3. 1821

    Read in London, then shelved

    Hardwicke's description is read at the Linnean Society on 6 November 1821.

  4. 1825

    Paris publishes first

    Anomaly

    Frédéric Cuvier describes Ailurus fulgens, the “shining cat,” from Alfred Duvaucel's specimens, which probably also came via Bharat Singh. First to print wins the name. Anomaly: “panda” now means the red animal, for about 44 years before the giant panda is named.

  5. 1827

    Six years in the drawer

    Delayed

    Hardwicke's paper finally appears in Transactions of the Linnean Society vol. 15. The name is already Cuvier's.

Where the two tracks meet

  1. 2021

    The coat is camouflage

    Open question

    A Scientific Reports study (Nokelainen, Scott-Samuel, Nie, Wei & Caro; Bristol, Jyväskylä and the Chinese Academy of Sciences) tests photos of wild pandas against predator vision: the black and white blends into shade, trunks, foliage and snow. An animal that hides from predators… and from artists. For 2,000 years.
