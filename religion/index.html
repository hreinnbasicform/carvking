Bears, Whips and the Beast: A Late-Night Read of the Bible

It started with one strange story about bears and ran until about 3 a.m. This is a write-up of that conversation: the parts of the Bible that Sunday school sands down, and what happens when you read them as documents written by real people, in real cities, under a real empire.

Not a sermon, not a debunking: an argument about reading. Where a reading is contested, we say so, and the references are there so you can check.

1 · 2 Kings 2:23–25

Elisha and the bears

Elisha has just watched his master Elijah taken up into heaven. He walks to Bethel, and a crowd comes out of the town and jeers at him: “Go up, baldhead! Go up, baldhead!” He turns, curses them in the name of the Lord, and two she-bears come out of the woods and maul forty-two of them. That's the whole story. Three verses.

The Sunday-school version, when told at all, is a lesson in manners: little kids teased a holy man and God punished them. Told that way it's horrible, which is why most children's Bibles skip it.

But look at the words. The Hebrew is ne'arim qetannim, usually translated “small boys,” and verse 24 calls the forty-two yeladim. The trouble is that na'ar stretches a long way. Joseph is a na'ar at seventeen (Gen 37:2). Absalom is called one while leading an armed revolt against his father (2 Sam 18:5). The word covers boy, teenager, young man or servant. And forty-two is not a playground. Forty-two is a mob.

Then there's the location. Bethel was the site of one of the northern kingdom's golden-calf shrines (1 Kings 12:28–29), the rival cult centre that the books of Kings treat as the original sin of Israel. A gang of young men pours out of that town to meet the new prophet of the Lord, and their taunt is “go up.” Many commentators hear that as a sneer at Elijah's ascension: your master went up, why don't you go too?

So the mainstream reading isn't “God kills kids for teasing.” It's a hostile crowd from a rival cult centre confronting the new prophet, with the outcome told as a verdict on Bethel. You don't have to like it. But it's about authority and rebellion, not name-calling.

2 · Gospels and Revelation

Jesus using force

The temple

In John's account, Jesus walks into the temple courts, finds the cattle and sheep sellers and the money changers, and makes a whip of cords (John 2:15). He drives out the animals, pours out the coins and overturns the tables. Braiding a whip takes time: this was premeditated, not a lost temper. Mark puts it in the last week of his life (Mark 11:15–17); John puts it at the start.

Legion and the pigs

Among the tombs in the region of the Gerasenes, on the gentile side of the lake, a man breaks every chain put on him. His spirits call themselves Legion, “for we are many.” Jesus sends them into about two thousand pigs, which rush into the sea and drown (Mark 5:1–20). A legion was a Roman army unit, and the argument goes that an audience under occupation wouldn't miss it: something called Legion occupies a man and a land, and gets thrown into the sea. Some add that the Tenth Legion used a boar as an emblem. Suggestive, not proven.

The fig tree

On the way into Jerusalem, Jesus finds a fig tree with leaves but no fruit and curses it. Next morning it has withered to the roots (Mark 11:12–14, 20–21). Mark sandwiches it around the temple scene, and most scholars read the tree as the temple establishment: all leaves, no fruit.

The sword in Gethsemane

When the arrest party comes, one of the disciples draws a sword and cuts off the ear of the high priest's servant. Only John names them: the swordsman is Peter and the servant is Malchus (John 18:10). Only Luke has Jesus touch the ear and heal it (Luke 22:50–51). Matthew adds “all who take the sword will perish by the sword” (Matt 26:52). Here, where force would only protect Jesus himself, he undoes it.

The rider on the white horse

And then Revelation 19. Heaven opens, a rider called Faithful and True comes out on a white horse, with eyes like fire and a robe dipped in blood, and strikes down the nations (Rev 19:11–16). Though the sword comes out of his mouth (19:15), which many read as the word, not steel.

The pattern we landed on

Force shows up when something is occupying territory that isn't its own: merchants in the house of prayer, a Legion inside a man, a barren tree standing where fruit should be, an empire sitting on the world. When the fight is only about protecting Jesus himself, the sword goes back in its sheath.

3 · Revelation 13 and 17

Revelation as a coded war letter against Rome

Here's the provocative bit. Revelation says plainly who it's addressed to: seven churches in the Roman province of Asia, in what is now western Turkey (Rev 1:4, 11). It says the events are coming “soon” (1:1; 22:10). Then it hands its readers a beast rising from the sea with seven heads and ten horns (13:1), and in chapter 17 it all but decodes it. The seven heads are “seven mountains on which the woman is seated” (17:9). Everyone knew which city sat on seven hills. The woman is “the great city that rules over the kings of the earth” (17:18): one candidate.

“Babylon” as a codename for Rome isn't a modern invention either. First Peter signs off from “Babylon” (1 Pet 5:13), and Jewish texts written after Rome destroyed the temple in AD 70, such as 4 Ezra, 2 Baruch and the Sibylline Oracles, do the same. Babylon destroyed the first temple; Rome became the new Babylon.

The heads are also kings (17:10), and one head has a mortal wound that has healed (13:3). That fits the rumour, widespread after Nero's suicide in AD 68, that Nero hadn't really died and would come back from the east. And the beast demands worship (13:4, 15). In Asia, the imperial cult was civic life.

On this reading, Revelation is resistance literature in code, telling a persecuted minority that the beast loses and Babylon falls (Rev 18). The argument goes that the “end times” reading is the cover story: it lets churches skip past the fact that this was mail to specific people under a specific emperor, and turn it into a puzzle about the evening news.

Where scholars push back

Rome-as-Babylon is close to consensus. Nero-as-the-beast is common but not universal. And the dating is genuinely disputed: Irenaeus placed the book under Domitian, around AD 95, and most scholars follow him, while full preterists argue for the late 60s under Nero. Futurist interpreters accept the Roman backdrop but argue the visions also point beyond it.

4 · Revelation 13:18

666, 616 and Nero Caesar

“Let anyone with understanding calculate the number of the beast, for it is the number of a person. Its number is six hundred sixty-six” (Rev 13:18). It practically begs for gematria: adding up the number values of letters.

Take the Greek Neron Kaisar and write it in Hebrew letters: נרון קסר, NRON QSR. That spelling is attested in an Aramaic document from Wadi Murabba'at dated to Nero's reign. Add it up:

NRV QSR502006—10060200616

Now the part that makes it more than a party trick. Some manuscripts read 616 instead of 666, including one of the oldest surviving fragments of Revelation, Papyrus Oxyrhynchus 115. Drop the final nun, spelling the name as the Latin Nero rather than the Greek Neron (NRV QSR), and you get exactly 616. One name, both numbers.

Irenaeus, writing around AD 180 (Against Heresies 5.30), knew both readings. He defended 666 and blamed 616 on copyists. To be fair, he didn't decode it as Nero; he floated other names. But he's our witness that both numbers circulated within a century.

Where scholars push back

Gematria is flexible. With enough freedom over spelling and language you can make almost anyone add up to 666, and people have done it to popes and presidents. And why would a Greek book use Hebrew letters? The reply is the double attestation: one name explaining both 666 and 616 is harder to wave away than a one-off match. Many critical scholars find Nero the strongest candidate, but it remains a scholarly argument, not a settled fact.

5 · Revelation 2–3

The seven churches: letters with addresses

Revelation 2–3 contains seven short letters, to Ephesus, Smyrna, Pergamum, Thyatira, Sardis, Philadelphia and Laodicea. The popular reading treats them as timeless messages to every church, or seven ages of church history. But look at a map: from the port of Ephesus north to Smyrna and Pergamum, then south-east through Thyatira, Sardis and Philadelphia to Laodicea. The classic argument (William Ramsay's) is that this is a courier's circuit on the Roman roads. And each letter knows its town.

  • Ephesus First stop. Praised for hard work and for testing false apostles, rebuked for abandoning “the love you had at first” (2:1–7).
  • Smyrna No rebuke at all, only “be faithful until death, and I will give you the crown of life” (2:8–11). Smyrna is where Polycarp, who according to Irenaeus had known John, was burned around AD 155. (Philadelphia is the only other church that also escapes criticism.)
  • Pergamum “I know where you are living, where Satan's throne is” (2:13). Candidates on that acropolis include the Great Altar of Zeus, a monumental throne-shaped altar, and the temple to Augustus and Roma, the province's first imperial-cult temple. A local martyr, Antipas, is named.
  • Thyatira A city of trade guilds (bronze-workers, dyers, wool-workers; Lydia, the seller of purple in Acts 16:14, came from there). Guild life meant banquets with meat offered to the gods. The letter attacks a local prophetess it nicknames “Jezebel” for teaching people to eat food sacrificed to idols (2:18–29).
  • Sardis The old capital of Lydia and of Croesus, the byword for wealth. “You have a name of being alive, but you are dead. Wake up” (3:1–2). Its “impregnable” citadel fell twice to surprise night attacks, to Cyrus and later to Antiochus III, because no one was watching. The letter warns: “I will come like a thief” (3:3).
  • Philadelphia Little power, but it kept the word, and gets no rebuke. Promised to be made “a pillar” and given “a new name” (3:7–13), in an earthquake-wrecked city that had briefly taken a new imperial name after the emperor helped rebuild it.
  • Laodicea The famous one. “Because you are lukewarm, and neither cold nor hot, I am about to spit you out” (3:16). Laodicea had no good water source of its own and piped it in by aqueduct. It arrived tepid and loaded with minerals. To the north, across the valley, Hierapolis had hot springs. To the east, Colossae had cold, fresh water. Hot heals, cold refreshes; lukewarm makes you gag. The letter also mocks the city's banking, black wool and eye-salve (“poor, blind, and naked,” 3:17–18), and Tacitus records it rebuilt after an earthquake around AD 60 without Roman help (Annals 14.27): “I am rich, I need nothing.”

Two readings

The “timeless” reading is right that these letters have been preached everywhere since. The local reading says they were first written to real congregations, with jabs that only land if you know the water supply and the skyline. Some links (the water especially) are debated, but together they read like letters with addresses.

6 · The preterist reading

What this does to John

If Revelation is about Rome, then its author changes shape. He stops being a fortune-teller and becomes a pastor in exile. “I, John, your brother who share with you in the persecution… was on the island called Patmos” (Rev 1:9). He's writing survival manuals to churches he knows by name.

The beast is Rome. And the mark of the beast, without which “no one can buy or sell” (13:16–17), starts to look less like a microchip and more like the price of taking part in the imperial economy: guild banquets with sacrificed meat, coins stamped with a divine emperor, the civic loyalty rituals that kept your business open. Thyatira's “Jezebel” is the same question at street level: how far do you go along to get along?

Then there's the strange double portrait. Tradition says the same John wrote the gentle Gospel of the Good Shepherd and the book of dragons and bowls of wrath. Whiplash, but human: a pastor can write tenderly about love and, when his people are being executed, furiously about the empire doing it. The pattern from section 2 again. (Fair warning: Dionysius of Alexandria argued in the 3rd century, from the Greek style, that these were different authors, and many modern scholars agree there were different Johns.)

So why did the future-prophecy reading win? The argument goes like this. Once the empire turned Christian, a book naming Rome as the beast became awkward. Pushing the beast into the future made the church's alliance with power look temporary-proof, not the next Babylon. Augustine spiritualised the millennium; 16th-century Jesuits (Ribera, Alcázar) built futurist and preterist systems partly to deflect Protestants calling the pope Antichrist; 19th-century dispensationalism gave us the rapture charts. Historians would call it messier than “suppression.” But the reading that lasted never asks the church to look in the mirror.

7 · The texts that didn't make it

What else got buried

The Gospel of Thomas

In December 1945, farmers near Nag Hammadi in Egypt dug up a jar of Coptic codices. Among them was a complete Gospel of Thomas: 114 sayings attributed to Jesus, with no birth story, no crucifixion and no resurrection narrative. Just “Jesus said.” Some sayings match the canonical Gospels; others are strange (“split a piece of wood; I am there”). Its date is argued, from the late 1st to the 2nd century. A Jesus remembered as a teacher rather than a sacrifice complicates the official story.

Nicaea, and what it didn't do

The popular story says the Council of Nicaea in 325 sat down and voted on which books went into the Bible. It didn't. Nicaea was convened by the emperor Constantine to settle the Arian controversy, whether the Son was fully divine and “of one substance” with the Father, and to fix the date of Easter. The emperor called it and attended, and losing bishops were exiled. The canon was settled more slowly. Athanasius' Easter letter of 367 is the first list matching our 27 New Testament books, and the North African councils of Hippo (393) and Carthage (397) endorsed a list. Less cinematic than the myth, but no less political: the canon wasn't decided in one vote. It was sorted out over a century by people with power to protect.

The Book of Enoch

The letter of Jude, which is in the Bible, quotes the Book of Enoch directly, introducing it as prophecy by “Enoch, in the seventh generation from Adam” (Jude 14–15, quoting 1 Enoch 1:9). 1 Enoch, the tale of the Watchers, angels who fathered giants, turned up in Aramaic among the Dead Sea Scrolls. Yet it's out of almost every Bible, except the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church's, where it's canonical today. A book good enough for an apostle to quote was not good enough for the canon.

The Dead Sea Scrolls

From 1947 on, caves near Qumran gave up the library of a Jewish sect that most scholars identify with the Essenes, though that identification is debated. Their rulebooks describe ritual immersion, shared property, a communal meal of bread and new wine with a messiah present (1QSa), and “sons of light” awaiting the end of the age, with priestly and royal messianic figures. One text (4Q521) says the Lord will heal the wounded, raise the dead and bring good news to the poor, nearly the same list Jesus gives John the Baptist (Matt 11:5). Another (4Q246) speaks of a figure called “Son of God” and “Son of the Most High.” It looks like early Christianity before Christianity existed. Much of it sat unpublished with a small team until the early 1990s, feeding every conspiracy theory going. (The mainstream explanation is slowness and territoriality, not a cover-up, and the scrolls never mention Jesus.)

The pattern

The stuff that got buried, whether in a jar, a cave or a footnote, tends to be the stuff that complicates the official story: a Jesus without a cross, a prophecy an apostle quoted, a sect that looked Christian before Christ. You don't have to believe in a conspiracy to notice which way the filtering ran.
