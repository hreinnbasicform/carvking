The 2026 Midterms: Money, Musk, and the Fight for Congress

On November 3, 2026, Americans vote in the midterm elections. Republicans are defending their majorities in both the House and the Senate. Democrats are favored in the polls, but Republicans hold a massive cash advantage.

This page is a documentary record of the cycle as it stood in the final days of September 2026: who is paying for it, what Elon Musk is saying and spending, where the Democratic money comes from, how the impeachment debate is cutting, and what real-money prediction markets expect. Section 2

Elon Musk's role

Musk is both the cycle's loudest online voice on election integrity and one of its largest individual funders. His posts on X in the week of September 23–30, 2026 returned repeatedly to voter ID, immigration and noncitizen voting.

What he posted, September 23–30

  • Sept 27“In New York and California, they have made it illegal for officials to ask for or for you to show your voter ID! They made election fraud mandatory.” Posted in response to his mother Maye Musk's account of her experience at the polls.
  • Sept 26 †“This is exactly what they will do. After that, America will become a one-party state.” Quoting an RNC Research clip of Rep. Tom Suozzi on mass amnesty.
  • Sept 25“Even this alone makes @DOGE worth it.” On the Supreme Court greenlighting use of the federal citizenship database for voter verification.
  • Sept 23 †“Democrats, which is why they push for open borders and noncitizen voting! Incentives explain behavior.”

America PAC: the field program

On July 30, the New York Times reported that Musk had authorized America PAC to spend $100–120 million on a field program, led by his adviser Chris Young. The plan centres on door-knocking, digital and direct mail aimed at low-propensity conservative voters: people who lean Republican but often skip midterms.

  • Senate targets: Alaska, Iowa, Maine, Michigan and Ohio, with North Carolina, Georgia and Texas also under consideration.
  • House races: California, Wisconsin and Washington.
  • Allies: coordinating with Americans for Prosperity and Sentinel Action Fund.†

The money trail

America PAC reported roughly $274,000 in cash on hand at June 30 in its FEC quarterly report. It had raised $50.3 million for the cycle and spent it down. September FEC filings then showed a sharp ramp-up: about $20 million deployed.

Section 4

The Democratic side

Democrats trail badly in party and super PAC cash but have a strong candidate-fundraising and small-dollar base, and they lead in the polls.

Where the big Democratic money comes from

  • The Soros family is the single largest donor of the cycle on either side at $103.3M, mostly routed through Democracy PAC (see table above).
  • Senate Majority PAC held about $126M cash at the end of June. With its affiliated nonprofit Majority Forward it raised $147M in Q2 2026, $326M combined for the cycle. It has booked $54M more in TV, targeting Ohio ($60M total), Iowa ($30.5M), North Carolina ($42.5M) and Alaska ($11M).†
  • House Majority Forward / Majority Forward nonprofits: about $61–62M each.†
  • Reid Hoffman: about $15M via Lone Star Rising PAC for James Talarico in Texas.†
  • JB Pritzker: about 80% of the funding for a super PAC spending about $9M for Juliana Stratton in Illinois.†

Candidates, party and grassroots

  • Democratic candidates have outspent their Republican opponents by $48M in direct campaign funds; Talarico alone has spent $47M.†
  • The DNC has $16.9M cash against $17.6M debt. Grassroots giving is strong: $14M in July and August from 1 million unique donors.†
  • Democrats lead the generic ballot by about 7 points and are favored in most battleground polls.†

The shape of the race, then: Republicans have more outside money and more ad time booked; Democrats have better candidate fundraising, more grassroots donors, and a better national environment.

Section 5

Why Democrats Can't Win: The Impeachment Question

Republicans argue that open talk of a third impeachment is a gift to them: it lets them frame the midterms as a vote on two more years of investigations rather than on prices and policy. Many Democrats take the opposite view and say accountability is the point. Here is what both sides have said, in their own words.

The case for impeachment

“No, it's not [a mistake]. Because it's not about Donald Trump, he's going to be a lame duck and, in my view, largely increasingly irrelevant. The point is to stand up for the Constitution.”

“Impeachment says that you can't get into an illegal war in Iran without constitutional approval. Impeachment says that you can't just start firing federal employees in defiance of what Congress says. Impeachment says you can't do deals with foreign governments to allegedly enrich your own family. It's about setting a standard.”

Rep. Ro Khanna (D-CA), NBC's Meet the Press, September 27, 2026

“Trump is clearly the most impeachable President our country has ever endured. His continued presence in office is a danger to our democracy.”

Rep. Steve Cohen (D-TN), introducing 26 articles of impeachment, September 24, 2026

“We haven't ruled anything in or ruled anything out in terms of accountability.” †

House Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries (D-NY), repeatedly

“If that's not high crimes and misdemeanors - and if it's not madness - I don't know what is.” †

Rep. Madeleine Dean (D-PA), on Trump's Iran threat

“threatening genocide is not just against international law, it's against our federal law, too.”

Rep. Sara Jacobs (D-CA), on Trump's Iran threat

“Congress needs to get back to work and remove him from office before he does more damage.”

Rep. Seth Moulton (D-MA), on Trump's Iran threat

Sen. Andy Kim (D-NJ) has also pushed for impeachment, saying Trump “is unfit to be the Commander in Chief” and “should be removed.”†

The Republican warning

“They say they're not going to, but we know they will—they'll do a third impeachment of President Trump. But that's just part of it. They're going to investigate the first family, they're going to investigate Cabinet secretaries.”

Rep. Jim Jordan (R-OH), House Judiciary Committee Chair, radio interview, September 27, 2026

The Democratic counterpoint

“Don't be impeaching anybody. Let's just get back to work.”

Sen. Tammy Duckworth (D-IL)

Duckworth is not alone. Many establishment Democrats fear that impeachment talk alienates the swing voters the party needs, and that it hands Republicans a closing message. For a party leading on the generic ballot, the risk is less that impeachment talk costs it the House than that it narrows the margin in the Senate states where Trump ran strongest.

Analysis / opinion

Grok's Take (as of September 30, 2026)

This subsection is analysis and opinion, not reporting.

House at 91–93%: reasonable, arguably even conservative

The structural factors are overwhelming. The president's party has lost House seats in every midterm since World War II except 1998 and 2002. Democrats lead the generic ballot by about 7 points. Republicans hold only a 221–214 majority† and Democrats need a net gain of 4 in a tough environment. Trump's approval is deep underwater (about 38–39%†). The only realistic paths to a Republican House are a major external shock (war, crisis) or a late polling miss. Markets have been stable here for weeks, and that lack of movement is itself a sign of confidence.

Senate at 62–63%: the market's soft spot

It looks reasonable on the surface but sits above several fundamentals-based models: Scrutinel simulations give Democrats 56%,† and VotePredictor's model gives Republicans 55%.† The gap suggests the market may be overweighting recent polling momentum and underweighting the map. Democrats must flip seats in states Trump won by 10+ points: Texas, Ohio, Alaska, Iowa and Maine. A 62% price is a lean, not a lock; 2024 showed Polymarket can move 15+ points in the final six weeks. Watch Iowa, Maine, Ohio and Texas. Kalshi's own “closest race” market has Texas at 16%, Iowa 13% and Kansas 11%.†

What would move the odds

  1. A major geopolitical or economic shock before November 3. This is the single biggest wildcard, as it was for Trump in 2024.
  2. October polling. If the generic ballot tightens below 4 points, House odds compress fast.
  3. Musk's October 15 FEC filing. If America PAC's Q3 numbers show the $100–120M field program is real and deployed, Republican turnout models improve, especially in Iowa, Maine and Ohio, where door-knocking matters most.
  4. Candidate-specific news in the four toss-up Senate states.
  5. A Democratic messaging stumble on impeachment. The Khanna/Cohen/Jeffries impeachment talk is a live risk factor the market hasn't fully priced.

Bottom line

The House is priced about right. The Senate is priced a touch rich for Democrats relative to the simulation models, and that's where the residual uncertainty lives.

Section 7

Closing: cash versus the polls

The 2026 midterms come down to a contest between two advantages. Republicans have the money: more party cash, a nine-figure pro-Trump super PAC, more ad time booked through Election Day, and a Musk-funded turnout operation aimed at the voters who usually stay home in midterms. Democrats have the environment: a lead on the generic ballot, stronger candidate and grassroots fundraising, and prediction markets that make them heavy favorites for the House and modest favorites for the Senate.

The next hard data point is October 15, when Q3 FEC filings will clarify Musk's final number and show whether America PAC's field program is running at the scale reported in July. The final one is Election Day, November 3, 2026.
